The Rising Cost of Judgment

Didier GuislandNovember 2026

During my time at UC Berkeley, I met Jeff Eyet, who was my professor and mentor. In his classes, Jeff often tells the story of what happened when the spreadsheet arrived in offices in the early 1980s: people, a little scared, expected that accountants would no longer be needed, but the curious thing is that exactly the opposite happened.

Bookkeeping clerks, the most routine job, barely changed in number, while accountants nearly doubled,1 because the spreadsheet hadn’t eliminated the work: it had moved it. The work stopped being arithmetic and became what had always been truly valuable: understanding and deciding what the numbers meant.

But this story Jeff shares isn’t about more work for accountants, or about how marvelous spreadsheets are. It’s about exploring a pattern that is repeating itself, and on an even bigger scale, with artificial intelligence (AI). For Jeff, AI is doing to the first draft what the spreadsheet did to arithmetic: producing a text, a design, or an analysis that looks well done now costs almost nothing.

And that’s where an even bigger question comes up: if today it’s so easy to produce answers, where does the real human value lie?

For Jeff, that value comes from two places. First, from “the question,” because to get a right answer we have to start by knowing what to ask. And then, from “the critique,” because understanding and attacking what the AI gave back is the difference between pure noise and finished work.

From there comes his most practical rule: a task is only worth automating if verifying the result costs less than producing it. And he sums it up in one line: “Answers are now free. Judgment isn’t.”2

With that line in mind, and turning it over and over, I asked myself: if answers keep getting cheaper and judgment keeps getting more expensive, then before automating or delegating, the question isn’t “What can AI do?” but “How much does it cost to get it wrong, and where do we need to be to prevent it?”

After a while, and with a lot of work along the way, what I call the “Vernuli Matrix” was born.

The idea is simpler than the name. Before automating or delegating a process, we try to break it down into concrete actions, like reading an email and writing a summary, entering an order and generating the shipment, or approving a payment and issuing an invoice. And we ask each one three questions.

The first is: What kind of solution does it call for? Not everything needs AI. If the information reaches us already organized and the rule is clear, a simple integration is enough. As Jeff puts it, AI makes sense when the information arrives messy but the result has a fixed shape, something we can compare it against afterward.

The second is: How much does making a mistake cost? There are mistakes that, before causing damage, pass through a point where someone can actually catch them. And there are others that go straight to a point of no return: sending, paying, publishing. Keep in mind that the cost can take different forms depending on the process: it can be financial, reputational, or even legal.

The third is: How much does reviewing cost? That is, how much time someone with good judgment has to put in to make sure the result is reliable.

With these three answers, we try to have each action land somewhere in the Matrix: automate it, automate it with a person at the right point, redesign it, or leave it as it is. But what we’re really looking for is that right point: the exact place in each process where you can’t let go of human judgment. Later on I’ll tell you how I found it myself.

But first I want to mention something: finding that point isn’t enough. Putting a person there guarantees nothing if their judgment can’t work. Let me tell you a bit more.

Researching cases of companies that have already automated with AI and live with it in their day-to-day operations, I realized that what we call judgment needs three conditions, and if any one of them is missing, it becomes a mere formality.

The first condition is having a source the AI doesn’t have. It sounds obvious, but it’s what gets overlooked the most. When someone reviews what the AI did, the real question isn’t whether the text reads well, since the AI almost always, if not always, makes it read well, but whether what it says is true. And to know whether something is true, that person needs something to compare it with: having been there, knowing the client, having the original data, or even what experience gives them. If they don’t have it, all they can check is spelling and tone, and the error slips right past them, neatly packaged.

Morgan Stanley understood this well. Its financial advisors use a tool that listens to their meetings with clients and gives them a summary, and one of them said it saves him about half an hour per meeting.3 But the summary isn’t reviewed by just anyone: it’s reviewed by the same advisor who was sitting in the meeting. The advisor knows whether the client said they wanted to take more risk or less, whether an inheritance came up or not, whether the summary exaggerated something. If that same summary were reviewed by someone from another department who never heard the conversation, they would see a flawless summary and approve it, even if it were wrong. Judgment works because the person brings something the machine doesn’t have.

The second condition is having time to use that source. Here we go back to Jeff’s rule: a task is only worth automating if verifying the result costs less than producing it. When reviewing costs almost the same as doing the work, two things happen, and neither is good: either the savings disappear, or people, pressed for time, start to just skim.

The Anchorage police, in Alaska, experienced this firsthand. They tested a tool that wrote officers’ reports from their body-camera recordings. On paper it sounded great: hours of paperwork done in minutes. In practice, every draft had to be read in full, corrected, and filled in with everything the recording didn’t capture, like what the officer saw that nobody said out loud, or what they knew about the place. In the end, reviewing and correcting ate up much of the time they were supposed to save, the savings never came, and they decided not to adopt it, at least for now.4 And the most interesting part is that they made the right decision. The dangerous thing would have been to keep the tool and ask the officers to review in two minutes what needed twenty. That’s where judgment stops existing, even if on paper it’s still there.

The third condition is having the authority to hit the brakes. You can have the right person, with the right information and enough time, and it’s still useless if they don’t have the power, or the standing, to stop things before they reach that point of no return we talked about in the Vernuli Matrix.

The clearest example is a Chevrolet dealership in California that put a chatbot on its website to answer customers’ questions. A user, just playing around, convinced it to agree with anything he said, and the chatbot ended up agreeing in writing to sell him a new SUV for $1.5 The problem wasn’t that the AI made a mistake; that was going to happen sooner or later. The problem was that the process was set up so the chatbot answered the customer directly, and between the two there was nobody with the authority to say “this doesn’t go out” before the reply arrived. They pulled it when the case was already circulating on social media, that is, when the damage to their image was already done. Seeing the error is useless if you can’t stop the machine.

And if we put the three together, the human judgment we’re talking about isn’t just putting a person in the process. It’s learning to put them in the right place, with something the AI doesn’t know, with the time to use it, and with the power to say no. If any of the three is missing, what’s left is a formality that gives peace of mind, but protects nothing.

After analyzing these cases, and others, I arrived at what I believe are two conclusions, and both go back to the same question of the Matrix: where do we put judgment?

The first is that, just like with the spreadsheet, the work moves. The person stops producing and becomes the one who decides: they review, correct, add to it, and put their own stamp on it. But when the work moves, the question changes too. It’s no longer “Who makes the draft?” but “In what part of the process does judgment need to be for the result to actually be useful?” And that’s exactly the question almost nobody asks.

And from there comes my second conclusion, which is a bit more uncomfortable: when nobody asks that question, judgment ends up at the end by default. Everything gets automated and, before it goes out, someone gives it a once-over. And to be fair, reviewing at the end isn’t bad in itself; Morgan Stanley does it, and it makes sense, because the one reviewing is the same person who was in the meeting. The problem is when the end is the only place where there’s judgment, and the person gets there without those three conditions.

Because at the end the work already looks perfect and finished: well written, well presented, and sure of itself. Whoever reviews it arrives in a hurry, and no longer reads to question; they read to approve. And AI gets things wrong with the same confidence with which it gets them right.

CNET, a well-known tech outlet in the United States, published articles written with AI that went through an editor; it later paused the experiment and had to correct 41 of the 77.6 Lawyers at a law firm filed court papers citing cases that never existed, made up by AI with all the formatting of real ones.7 A global consulting firm delivered a report with errors to a government and had to refund part of its fee.8

In all three cases there was, or there was supposed to be, someone reviewing at the end. And in all three, the error was found by an outsider. The problem wasn’t that a person was missing; it was where they put them and what they gave them to work with.

So where does it go, then? That’s something I didn’t fully understand by reading cases. I understood it by doing.

And here I go back to what I was telling you about the Vernuli Matrix, to that right point we’d talk about later, because where I realized it most was through a personal experience: building my own video factory.

It started with a concrete goal: putting out reels every week for a health brand on social media, almost on autopilot, and learning from each one with real data. I built it as an assembly line of AI agents: one proposed the topic, another wrote the script, another handled art direction, another animated and another reviewed.

And the curious thing is that, at first, I made exactly the same mistake as many companies. The first tests turned out so well that I was dazzled: I wanted everything to be automatic, fast, and cheap, and I trusted the AI with everything. All I said was “make me a video,” and whatever I didn’t like, I corrected at the end, right in that default place I was telling you about.

The structure worked, but the videos came out technically correct but flat: without the hook I was looking for, with too many details, some of them out of place. And what I’d wanted to be cheap turned out to be expensive: redoing a finished animation cost about $7; making the same change in the storyboard, about $1.20.9

So I put the three questions of the Matrix to my own factory. What kind of solution does each step call for? Syncing the voice or running the render has a fixed shape, so there the machine alone is enough. How much does a mistake cost? In the idea and the hook, everything: if those fail, the whole video comes out soulless. And how much does reviewing cost? In the storyboard, almost nothing; in the finished animation, six times more. That was my right point, and I kept shifting where I stood in the process. But to be clear, that doesn’t mean I now do everything. I don’t write the idea or the script. The agents propose topics, hooks, and stories, and I become part of that conversation: I ask, I question, I choose, I ask for another round, until the idea, the hook, and the script are locked in. Then I review the storyboard and a few test shots. The rest I let go: the voice syncs on its own, the animation is coded, and the render runs without me. I didn’t stop delegating. I stopped delegating blindly.

And along the way I learned something else: every time I correct something, the AI writes it down in a log of corrections, along with how many times each one recurs. The reviewing agent uses it in the next round, so I don’t correct the same thing twice. Each correction stops being a fix and becomes learning.

Now, I want to make something very clear: this isn’t only for something creative like a video factory, or for big automations. That same dazzle I felt hits any company that puts its people to work with AI: at first everything turns out so well that the temptation is to let go of everything. But what really changes the result isn’t how much you delegate to AI but whether the person stops just producing and becomes part of the process, in a conversation with the machine. I’ve started to see the same thing at Vernuli, the consultancy I co-founded, from our very first project: what matters most isn’t how much technology you use, but understanding when someone has to step in.

And for that you don’t need to know how to code. In the United Kingdom, in a study of 68 schools, teachers who used ChatGPT to prepare their lessons spent 56 minutes a week on it, compared with 81 for those who didn’t use it, and an expert panel found no difference in the quality of their lessons:10 the AI proposed, but the teacher decided what worked for their class. At Procter & Gamble, commercial and R&D professionals who worked with AI, even on their own, came up with product proposals as good as those of a full team without it:11 the AI helped explore ideas, but the business knowledge came from them. And in Kenya, in a network of clinics, an AI assistant reviews the doctors’ notes and sends them alerts, but the decision is still theirs; across almost 40,000 visits, they made 16 percent fewer diagnostic errors in a first study, and a later trial, published in *Nature Medicine*, found the tool safe.12 None of them coded anything. They simply knew where in the conversation they needed to be.

And this is where I’d add something to Jeff’s idea. Judgment doesn’t live only before, in the question, or only after, in the critique. It also lives in the middle, in the turns along the way.

Working with AI is like riding in a self-driving car. The machine picks the route and drives much of the way on its own, but there are turns where someone has to take the wheel, because only the person inside knows that the plans have changed, or senses that something on the road doesn’t look right. That said, it’s not about deciding every street, but about knowing which turns matter.

In my factory, those turns aren’t at the beginning or at the end: they’re in that conversation with the agents, in the storyboard and in the test shots. That’s where I don’t let go of the wheel.

And those turns matter more today than ever. When producing was slow, an error had time to be noticed; someone saw it along the way. Today AI multiplies it in minutes, well written and with all the confidence in the world. There are fewer moments where a person needs to be there, yes, but in each one of them everything is at stake.

That’s why I talk about a rising cost. It’s not that thinking costs more than it used to; it’s that what’s abundant gets cheaper and what’s scarce goes up in price. Answers are everywhere. What isn’t is someone with judgment, at the right turn, in time. And getting that turn wrong costs more every day.

If we go back to where we started, history repeats itself. When the spreadsheet arrived, accountants didn’t disappear: they stopped doing arithmetic and became the ones who decided what the numbers meant. Today the same thing is happening to us, but faster and to everyone. The question is no longer whether AI can do your job. The question is whether you know which turn nobody else can take for you.

That’s why the advantage won’t go to whoever automates the most. It will go to whoever knows how to find those turns, puts someone there with real judgment (a source of their own, the time to use it, and the authority to say no), and turns every correction into learning.

Answers are now free. Judgment, more expensive by the day.

And that’s exactly why the most valuable thing today isn’t knowing how to use AI, but knowing where not to let go of the wheel.
